Leave-one-out solver analysis · CoW Protocol

Every mainnet solver removed in turn, and the quotes with them

Window: 2026-07-01 → 2026-08-01, every mainnet auction in it re-arbitrated with one solver removed — 26 solvers, one scenario each, 83,621 of 85,487 auctions analysed. The quote competition is re-run for the same solvers over 2026-07-22 → 2026-08-01, which is as far back as the quote data goes. Every figure in the tables is a total over its window, not a rate.

What was measured: each auction’s winner selection is re-run on the recorded bids with one solver’s bids removed, and the outcome compared with what actually happened. The same solver is removed from the quote competition, so the price the user was shown moves too; what that does to the price-improvement fee is reported in the quote table and kept out of the fee column, which is computed with the recorded quotes on both sides. Signs: every delta is counterfactual − actual — negative Δsurplus means users would have received less without the solver, and negative Δfees means the protocol would have collected less. Settlement: a replacement winner inherits the on-chain outcome of the recorded winner whose token pairs it claims, so a batch that really reverted stays reverted whoever wins it; settlement cancels out of Δsurplus and what the figures measure is the competition’s decision, not its execution. USD figures are display-only conversions at each auction’s own stablecoin-implied rate.

Summary

  • Rizzolver is the largest on the solve side: −$184,152 of user surplus (−101.80 ETH) over the window, −$118,358 of DAO fee revenue, and 1,687 order executions that would not have happened — 1.6% of every user-order execution the chain traded.
  • Fractal follows at −$29,998 and Quasi at −$29,528, then Tsolver at −$22,154 and Sector at −$17,887. Fractal carries the largest coverage figure of any solver, 5,741 executions (5.6%).
  • On the quote side 15 of the 26 solvers won a matched quote, Curve exactly one. Tsolver won the most, 10,182, with a median degradation of −29.80 bps; Flowdesk’s median is −24.25 bps on 139 wins and Helixbox’s −21.94 bps on 493. Baseline’s removal would leave the most orders unquoted, 41, with Tsolver next at 37.
  • Every quote-side average is heavy-tailed. Fractal’s −4.97 bps over all executed volume is 86.2% one order and its median is −7.55 bps; Baseline’s −0.04 bps is 64.4% one order. The median and the concentration share are printed beside every figure for that reason, and replacement quotes more than twice or less than half the recorded rate are excluded as broken estimates rather than averaged in.
  • The fee column is computed with the recorded quotes on both sides and is negative for every solver. The quote’s own fee effect runs the other way and is its own column in the quote table: without Rizzolver the DAO would lose $118,358 of fee on the solve side while the same executions, recharged against worse quotes, would pay +$93,614 more (and +$48,253 to partners); Kipseli −$6,706 against +$21,062, Wraxyn −$1,857 against +$20,641. The two are never added on this page.

The window, one row per solver

The four left columns are the measures this page reports: Δsurplus in USD with the native amount beneath; Δprice, the same surplus change spread over all volume the chain traded, with the still-traded conditional reading beneath; Δprotocol and partner fees, the gross the mechanism charges, split into the protocol’s and the partners’ shares beneath; and the share of all executed orders that would not have traded. The two muted columns to the right carry supporting detail. Definitions are in the footnote, limits in the caveats.

Solver Δsurplus Δprice, all traded volume Δprotocol and partner fees Orders no longer executed, share of all Won Largest auction share

“Δsurplus” is the change in user surplus, summed over the window. “Δprice, all traded volume” spreads the moved-order Δsurplus over the window’s whole executed fill-or-kill volume, so unmoved orders count as zero; its sub-line gives the conditional reading — the change on just the still-traded orders whose price moved, with the median moved order and the moved count. “Δprotocol and partner fees” is the whole fee the winning solutions charged, split beneath into what the protocol keeps and what passes to partners, with the auctions whose fees could not be priced noted at the end; both are summed over every order either side traded, so an order that stops trading contributes its whole fee. This column is computed with the recorded quotes on both sides: it is what the removal does to fees through who executes. The quote’s own fee effect is its own column in the next section, and the two are not added anywhere on this page. “Orders no longer executed” divides the orders only the solver executed by every user-order execution the chain’s recorded winners traded in the window, partially fillable included; an order counts once per auction it executes in. “Won” is the solver’s share of analysed auctions, with its bid rate beneath. “Largest auction share” is a concentration check on the Δsurplus column and on nothing else: the single largest auction’s |Δsurplus| as a share of the row’s total, over the auctions where surplus moved at all — read the caveat before quoting a row above 30%. The fee cell carries the same check on its own column beneath the split (“largest auction”), because a single recharged order can be most of a quoting solver’s fee delta. Price-suspect auctions (1,146) and auctions with unrecoverable JIT order data (720) are excluded from every figure and enumerated in the report files. Solver rewards move under these scenarios too and are reported in the comparison output; they are not shown here.

The quote competition

The quote competition prices orders into existence: it decides the price the user was shown, which becomes the order’s limit price and the baseline every price-improvement fee is charged against. Removing a solver from it changes all three. Three figures follow, one per question — how much worse the quotes would have been, how often there would have been no quote at all, and what that does to the price-improvement fee, which is a column here and is kept out of Δprotocol and partner fees above.

Three facts about the window bound every figure in this table. The re-run pick returns the estimator the competition actually chose on 99.53% of 38,043 matched requests (the 326 whose recorded token price fails the cross-check below are left out of the check), and the rule that a verified quote outranks an unverified one at any amount held on 14,810 of 14,810 of the requests that carried both. Of the 38,396 orders created in the quote window that carry a recorded quote, 38,375 are linked to the competition that priced them — 17,751 through the request table and 20,624 through competitions that table never received, recovered from the raw estimate events — and 37,341 of those are confirmed by the winning estimate’s own amount; the 326 whose two native price feeds disagree leave 38,070 in every denominator. And the quote window is 2026-07-22 → 2026-08-01, ten days of the run’s thirty-one, because that is where the quote data begins.

Solver Δquote price, all executed volume Δquote price, median moved order Orders that would not have been quoted Largest order share Δprotocol fee from the quote

“Δquote price” is the change in the effective quote — the quoted amount net of the estimator’s own gas cost — spread over the executed volume of the window’s quoted orders, what those orders actually traded rather than what was quoted, so an order whose quote did not move, or that never traded, counts as zero; the sub-line gives the conditional reading over the orders that did move. Wins where the removed solver was the only verified estimator are excluded from both figures and counted on the sub-line: the replacement’s nominal amount can be higher while the quote is worse in kind, and a signed average would report that as an improvement. Wins whose replacement quote is more than twice, or less than half, the recorded quote’s rate are excluded as broken estimates promoted to winner — the ladder holds answers with a token’s decimals mis-scaled, and once the winner is gone such an answer wins; their counts are in the report files (69 over this window). Only matches corroborated by the winning estimate’s amount feed these figures or a fee. “Orders that would not have been quoted” are requests where the removed solver was the only usable estimator, so the order would never have been created; the count of orders lost to a removal is therefore this figure and the solve-side coverage column, and the two overlap. “Δprotocol fee from the quote” is the price-improvement fee the counterfactual executions would pay against the replacement quotes minus what they pay against the recorded ones — the DAO’s part, in USD at each auction’s rate, with the partners’ part, the count of recharged orders and the auctions holding them (including auctions the solver never bid in, since the fee reference moved there all the same) and the largest single order’s share beneath. It is positive for a solver whose quotes were good: without it the baseline the fee is measured against falls, and the same executions pay more. It is not added to the fee column above. The 326 orders whose two native price feeds disagree are excluded from every quote figure; wins where the gas cost exceeded the quoted amount are counted per solver in the report files and excluded. Quote rewards (CIP-72) are not evaluated on either side.

Protocol fee and partner fee

The competition ranks solutions on score, which is user surplus plus protocol fees, so a removal changes fee revenue as well as surplus. The fee figures are recomputed from each auction’s recorded fee policies and reproduce the protocol’s own recorded score exactly on the solutions where both are available.

“Protocol fee” is used here in the budget sense: what the protocol keeps. The analytics database and the protocol’s own code use the same words for the gross amount including the partners’ share, so the two are labelled separately — Δprotocol and partner fees for the gross, Δprotocol fee for the remainder.

The partners' share of the change runs from about zero to 16% across these solvers (BitgetWallet 16%, Rizzolver 12%, Kipseli 11%, Tsolver 10%). Partner fees are charged on volume, so they are unaffected when a rival wins the same order at a worse price and are lost only when the order does not trade. The partners' share of a fee delta therefore reads out how much of the delta is coverage rather than price. It is not the partners' share of the fee level, which is larger.

The fee column does not carry the quote effect. A worse quote lowers the baseline the price-improvement fee is measured against, so the same execution pays more fee, and folding that into the column would show the protocol gaining from losing a good quoter. It is reported instead as “Δprotocol fee from the quote” in the quote table, computed on the counterfactual executions, and the two figures are never added on this page. The recharged-order counts run from 28 to 7,932 across the fourteen quoting solvers with a recharge and are in the quote table, with the auctions holding them beneath — many of them auctions the solver never bid in (3,061 of Tsolver’s 7,059, 1,509 of Kipseli’s 3,312), because the quote winner often does not bid on its own order. The recharge to the DAO runs from +$90 (Flowdesk) to +$93,614 (Rizzolver), +$158,221 over the fourteen, and for Rizzolver, Kipseli and Wraxyn it is larger in magnitude than the solve-side fee loss — Rizzolver’s −$118,358 of solve-side DAO fee against +$93,614 of recharge, with a further +$48,253 to partners. That is why the two are kept apart: the recharge is what the mechanism would charge against worse quotes, not revenue the protocol would gain by losing a quoter. The largest single order is 38% of Wraxyn’s recharge and 8% of Rizzolver’s; the fee column’s own largest-auction share is at most 30% (Flowdesk) and 17% for Tsolver.

Reading each solver

Rizzolver

The largest row on every solve-side measure: −$184,152 of user surplus over 12,309 changed auctions, −$118,358 of DAO fee revenue (−$134,559 gross, −$16,201 of it partner fees), 1,687 executions that would not have traded. Its Δsurplus is not concentrated — the largest single auction is 3% of the row — so the figure is a run rate rather than an anecdote. On the quote side it won 4,333 matched quotes, degrading by a median −2.77 bps, with 4.4% of the weighted figure in one order — the least concentrated quote row on the page — and its quote recharge, +$93,614 to the DAO and +$48,253 to partners, is the largest on the page; it sits in the quote table and is not netted against the fee column.

Fractal

−$29,998 of surplus and the largest coverage figure in the window: 5,741 executions, 5.6% of everything the chain traded. Its fee delta is −$32,850 gross and −$32,565 to the DAO, larger in magnitude than its surplus delta; its quote recharge is +$1,395. Under assume-settled its surplus figure moves furthest of any solver, to −$51,858, so its headline figure is the most sensitive on the page to the settlement rule. On the quote side, 2,143 wins with a median degradation of −7.55 bps; its −4.97 bps window figure is the largest on the page and 86.2% of it is one order at −1,894 bps.

Tsolver

The solver with the most matched quote wins, 10,182, and the most recharged orders, 7,932 in 7,059 auctions, 3,061 of them auctions it never bid in; its median quote degradation, −29.80 bps, is the largest on the page. Its solve-side row is −$22,154 of surplus, −$9,254 of DAO fee and 1,255 executions; the quote recharge, +$10,806 to the DAO, is in the quote table. 37 orders would not have been quoted at all without it, second only to Baseline.

Wraxyn

The row where the quote recharge is largest relative to everything else: −$2,874 of Δsurplus and −$1,857 of solve-side DAO fee against +$20,641 of recharge, because the 1,057 recharged orders whose quotes it won would have been priced worse and the same executions would then have paid more price-improvement fee; 38% of that recharge is one order. Its −2.59 bps window figure is 61.6% one order at −4,846 bps, and the median is −0.72 bps.

Baseline

Small on the solve side — −$1,549 of surplus — and the solver whose removal leaves the most orders unquoted: 41 of 38,070, where no other solver exceeds 37. Its 9,281 quote wins degrade by a median −0.46 bps and its window figure is −0.04 bps, 64.4% of it one order.

The eleven solvers with no quote row

Quasi, Portus, Arc, TrustedVolumes, Rosato, OKX, AlgoLabs, Arctic, NativeFi, Odos and the uncatalogued 0x26a6fd… won no matched quote in the window. That is a finding, not missing data: they compete in the solve competition only. Quasi is the third-largest solve-side row in the window at −$29,528. Curve won exactly one, an order that would not have been quoted without it.

Caveats

  1. No behavioural response. Rivals’ bids and quotes are held fixed; nobody re-quotes or re-routes because a competitor is absent. This matters most where the figures are largest. The per-solver cap on solutions is applied before winners are picked, so a solution suppressed by the cap cannot step in either.
  2. Settlement is inherited, and that is an assumption. Winning an auction does not guarantee the batch lands on-chain, and a winner that never won for real never settled either, so the counterfactual has to decide what a replacement’s orders do. It gives the replacement the recorded winner’s outcome, which is the only choice grounded in the record. The alternative — assuming everything lands in time on both sides — is computed alongside and moves the figures in both directions: Fractal moves furthest against the solver, from −$29,998 to −$51,858; Rizzolver from −$184,152 to −$194,479 and Portus from −$5,983 to −$14,537. In the other direction Barter moves from −$6,292 to −$3,938, Baseline from −$1,549 to −$1,124. Those figures are in the comparison output.
  3. The quote pick is reproduced, not recorded. The competition records which estimator won, not why, so the pick is re-derived from the recorded ladder and the order’s own gas and token prices. It returns the recorded winner on 99.53% of matched requests; the rest are counted and not otherwise adjusted. A window where that rate falls is a window where the protocol’s rule changed, and every quote figure in it would be suspect.
  4. The order-to-quote match is a heuristic, placed against two layers. Nothing in the analytics database records which quote request created an order, so orders are matched on tokens, kind, amount and time, and the match is called corroborated when the winning estimate’s amount reproduces the amount stored on the order. The competition tables are built from the request event, and a competition whose event never arrived is missing from them — the API-placed orders, and Trust Wallet’s placement-time quotes — so those are recovered from the raw estimate events, where every one of the 20,624 such matches is corroborated. 38,375 of 38,396 orders are linked; fast quotes are never a candidate, because the quote an order carries is always a non-fast competition. Only corroborated matches feed a headline figure or a fee.
  5. A counterfactual quote does not change who won. Solution scores are the recorded ones and are never recomputed, so a different quote changes what the mechanism charges on an execution, not which solution the competition picked. The quote’s fee effect is reported in the quote table and is not added to the fee column; the two never compose on this page. Everything the solvers did — bids, executions, ranking — is held as recorded, and the fee is recomputed in every auction that executed a quoted order, whether or not the solver bid there. The user effect of a worse quote is the shown-price change in the quote table, not a surplus recomputed against the limit the user would have signed: measured surplus rises exactly when the user is shown a worse price. Two checks stand behind holding executions fixed and are counted in the report files: recharged orders whose execution would not have cleared the limit the replacement quote implies, which can only happen where the replacement quoted more, and auctions where shifting every score by that limit change would have moved the winner set. Over this window the first check flags 14 recharged orders in total (BitgetWallet 9, Rizzolver 3, Wraxyn 2), all class flips; the second flags 166 of the 8,724 auctions holding a Tsolver replacement quote, 25 of Baseline’s 7,962, 17 of Rizzolver’s 4,086, 13 of Kipseli’s 5,341, two each for Fractal and Sector, one each for Wraxyn and Horadrim. Where those auctions are in the report files, the replacement quotes in them are ordinary (a median worst of −40 bps for Tsolver, none below −1,000 bps), so what moved the winner set was a small shift in an already close race, not an extreme quote; the flagged auction ids are in the report files.
  6. Conversion is out of scope. A worse quote means some users would not have placed the order at all, and there is no counterfactual for an order nobody created. Mainnet serves about 1.2 M quote requests a day against the 38,070 quoted orders created in the ten-day quote window, about 3.8 k a day, so any elasticity here would be a guess. The quote figures describe the price shown on orders that were created.
  7. USD is display-only. Converted per auction from the auction’s own stablecoin-implied native prices; nothing on the valuation or reward path consumes a USD number. Native amounts are the exact figures. Quote-side figures derive from a Float64 source and are not exact to the atom; the fee, surplus and reward figures are.
  8. “Protocol fee” here excludes the partners’ share. The database and the protocol’s own code use “protocol fee” for the gross amount; this page uses it for what the protocol keeps and names the gross figure “protocol and partner fees”. Reading the gross number as protocol revenue overstates it by whatever the partners take: for the rows with a material gross the partners take up to 16% of the change (BitgetWallet 16%, Rizzolver 12%). The quote recharge in the quote table has a partners’ part of its own, shown beneath it — for Rizzolver +$48,253, because some partner fee policies are price-improvement fees too — and it is kept apart from this column in the same way.
  9. Fee figures are gross revenue. The protocol also pays rewards for the competition, and the reward change usually runs opposite to the fee change. The fee figures here are therefore not the net budget effect of a removal.
  10. Fees are exact where they are priced, and the exceptions are counted. A fee cannot be computed when a solution the real competition filtered out wins the counterfactual, because policies are only recorded for solutions that were kept. Those auctions drop from both sides of the fee comparison and are counted in the table. Nothing is read as a fee of zero.
  11. “Orders no longer executed” is a share of everything the chain executed. The narrower per-auction reading — relative only to auctions the solver bid in — is in the report files; it overstates chain-level dependence for a selective bidder and is not shown here.
  12. Δprice comes in two conditionings, labelled. The headline spreads the moved-order Δsurplus over all executed fill-or-kill volume; the sub-line is the change on just the still-traded orders that moved. The two differ by up to two orders of magnitude and neither substitutes for the other. Rows with few moved orders are anecdotes rather than run rates — the uncatalogued solver 0x26a6fd… moved 5 orders and Arctic 9, against Tsolver's 3,181 — and the moved count is printed with every figure. Partially fillable orders are excluded from both, since their two sides can execute different amounts.
  13. What “largest auction share” does and does not tell you. It is the concentration of the Δsurplus column only — the biggest single auction’s |Δsurplus| over the row’s signed total, across the auctions where surplus moved. It says nothing about the coverage, price or quote columns, which concentrate differently; the fee column carries its own largest-auction share on its detail line, and a row whose deltas offset can exceed 100%. It is a prompt to open the auction, not a discount factor: a 100% row is one real auction, not a wrong number. What it tracks is how few auctions moved, not chain size. For this run the concentrated rows are mostly the thin ones: of the seven rows at or above 30%, five moved fewer than 600 orders, Barter (56%, 6,443 moved) and Baseline (36%, 7,335 moved) are the exceptions, and the three largest rows by Δsurplus — Rizzolver, Fractal, Quasi — sit at 3%, 7% and 17%. A solver thin on a busy chain gets a concentrated row there.
  14. Concentrated rows, listed. Flowdesk 80%, Barter 56%, Gnosis_BalancerSOR 47%, OKX 41%, Baseline 36%, Arctic 30%, and the uncatalogued 0x26a6fd… at 313% — that last one is a row whose auctions offset, not a wrong number. On the quote side the same check flags nine of the fifteen rows: Fractal 86%, Helixbox 66%, Baseline 64%, Wraxyn 62%, Barter 59%, BRRRolver 56%, BitgetWallet 44%, Gnosis_BalancerSOR 35% and Tsolver 34%.
  15. Exclusions are named, not silent. Price-suspect and missing-JIT auctions are excluded from every statistic and enumerated in the report files.
  16. One chain. These solvers bid on other chains too, and removing a solver from each chain is an independent counterfactual, so nothing here describes a solver’s value across the protocol. Quote data exists for mainnet and BNB Chain only.

Method: full re-arbitration per auction (fairness filter included) on recorded scores; executed amounts from proposed executions; “executed” means settled in time. Protocol fees recomputed from the recorded fee policies, validated against the recorded score and against the accounting model’s own partner/protocol split. The quote pick is re-derived from the recorded estimate ladder in ClickHouse, ranked on the effective quote netted in the sell token at the order’s own recorded gas and token price. Reports: 52 files, format 12, generated 2026-09-02. Produced by loo analyse --quotes / loo compare.
