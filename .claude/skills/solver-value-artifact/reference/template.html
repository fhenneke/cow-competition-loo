What was measured: each auction’s winner selection is re-run on the recorded bids with one solver’s bids removed, and the outcome is compared with what actually happened. Signs: every delta is counterfactual − actual — negative Δsurplus means users would have received less without the solver, and negative Δfees means the protocol would have collected less. Settlement: a replacement winner inherits the on-chain outcome of the recorded winner whose token pairs it claims, so a batch that really reverted stays reverted whoever wins it. “Δsurplus” is the change in user surplus, summed over the window. “Δprice, all traded volume” spreads the moved-order Δsurplus over the window’s whole executed fill-or-kill volume, so unmoved orders count as zero; its sub-line gives the conditional reading — the change on just the still-traded orders whose price moved, with the median moved order and the moved count. “Δprotocol and partner fees” is the whole fee the winning solutions charged, split beneath into what the protocol keeps and what passes to partners, with the auctions whose fees could not be priced noted at the end; both are summed over every order either side traded, so an order that stops trading contributes its whole fee. “Orders no longer executed” divides the orders only the solver executed by every user-order execution the chain’s recorded winners traded in the window, partially fillable included; an order counts once per auction it executes in. “Won” is the solver’s share of analysed auctions, with its bid rate beneath. “Largest auction share” is a concentration check on the Δsurplus column and on nothing else: the single largest auction’s |Δsurplus| as a share of the row’s total, over the auctions where surplus moved at all — read the caveat before quoting a row above 30%. Price-suspect auctions and auctions with unrecoverable JIT order data are excluded from every figure and enumerated in the report files (PLACEHOLDER counts). Solver rewards move under these scenarios too and are reported in the comparison output; they are not shown here.

Protocol fee and partner fee

The competition ranks solutions on score, which is user surplus plus protocol fees, so a removal changes fee revenue as well as surplus. The fee figures are recomputed from each auction’s recorded fee policies and reproduce the protocol’s own recorded score exactly on the solutions where both are available.

“Protocol fee” is used here in the budget sense: what the protocol keeps. The analytics database and the protocol’s own code use the same words for the gross amount including the partners’ share, so the two are labelled separately — Δprotocol and partner fees for the gross, Δprotocol fee for the remainder.

The partners’ share of the change ranges from PLACEHOLDER to PLACEHOLDER across these chains. Partner fees are charged on volume, so they are unaffected when a rival wins the same order at a worse price and are lost only when the order does not trade. The partners’ share of a fee delta therefore reads out how much of the delta is coverage rather than price. It is not the partners’ share of the fee level, which is larger.

Caveats

  1. No behavioural response. Rivals’ bids are held fixed; nobody re-quotes or re-routes because a competitor is absent. This matters most where the figures are largest — a 41% win share disappearing would attract entry, and nothing here models that. The per-solver cap on solutions is applied before winners are picked, so a solution suppressed by the cap cannot step in either.
  2. Settlement is inherited, and that is an assumption. Winning an auction does not guarantee the batch lands on-chain, and a winner that never won for real never settled either, so the counterfactual has to decide what a replacement’s orders do. It gives the replacement the recorded winner’s outcome, which is the only choice grounded in the record. The alternative — assuming everything lands in time on both sides — is computed alongside and moves the figures in both directions: PLACEHOLDER — quote the rows that move most, in each direction. Those figures are in the comparison output.
  3. USD is display-only. Converted per auction from the auction’s own stablecoin-implied native prices; nothing on the valuation or reward path consumes a USD number. Native amounts are the exact figures.
  4. “Protocol fee” here excludes the partners’ share. The database and the protocol’s own code use “protocol fee” for the gross amount; this page uses it for what the protocol keeps and names the gross figure “protocol and partner fees”. Reading the gross number as protocol revenue overstates it by whatever the partners take: PLACEHOLDER — the smallest and largest partner shares in the run.
  5. Fee figures are gross revenue. The protocol also pays rewards for the competition, and the reward change usually runs opposite to the fee change. The fee figures here are therefore not the net budget effect of a removal.
  6. Fees are exact where they are priced, and the exceptions are counted. A fee cannot be computed when a solution the real competition filtered out wins the counterfactual, because policies are only recorded for solutions that were kept. Those auctions drop from both sides of the fee comparison and are counted in the table (PLACEHOLDER per row). Nothing is read as a fee of zero.
  7. “Orders no longer executed” is a share of everything the chain executed. The narrower per-auction reading — relative only to auctions the solver bid in — is in the report files; it overstates chain-level dependence for a selective bidder and is not shown here.
  8. Δprice comes in two conditionings, labelled. The headline spreads the moved-order Δsurplus over all executed fill-or-kill volume; the sub-line is the change on just the still-traded orders that moved. The two differ by up to two orders of magnitude and neither substitutes for the other. Rows with few moved orders are anecdotes rather than run rates — PLACEHOLDER, name the thinnest row — and the moved count is printed with every figure. Partially fillable orders are excluded from both, since their two sides can execute different amounts.
  9. What “largest auction share” does and does not tell you. It is the concentration of the Δsurplus column only — the biggest single auction’s |Δsurplus| over the row’s signed total, across the auctions where surplus moved. It says nothing about the fee, coverage or price columns, which concentrate differently, and a row whose deltas offset can exceed 100%. It is a prompt to open the auction, not a discount factor: a 100% row is one real auction, not a wrong number. What it tracks is how few auctions moved, not chain size. Any solver thin on a busy chain gets a concentrated row there.
  10. Concentrated rows, listed. PLACEHOLDER — every row at 30% or more, by solver, with its share.
  11. Exclusions are named, not silent. Price-suspect and missing-JIT auctions are excluded from every statistic and enumerated in the report files.
  12. Quote rewards are out of scope — the analysis has no data on counterfactual quoting.
